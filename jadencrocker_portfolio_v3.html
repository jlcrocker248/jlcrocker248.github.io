About

A childhood in the shop,
a career built on what I learned there.

I grew up at Crocker's Collision Center — my family's autobody shop in Watertown, South Dakota. After school, on weekends, through every summer: that shop was where I spent my time, working alongside my grandfather, my dad, and my two brothers. It was less a job than a way of life.

By the time I was sixteen, I had bought a totaled car and rebuilt it entirely myself — welding the chassis, repairing the structure, refinishing the body. Not as an assignment, but because it was the natural next step in a place where understanding how things work was simply what you did.

What the shop gave me wasn't just skill with tools. It was an instinct for thinking about how systems interact on a vehicle — how a chassis decision affects suspension geometry, how a structural repair changes the way loads distribute through the car. That systems-level intuition has shaped everything I've done since.

When I left for university, I wanted the theory to match the intuition. That led me to physics at Creighton — the mathematical language for forces, fluid behavior, and material mechanics — and then to engineering at Washington University, and eventually to aerodynamics. Aero is the invisible system that touches everything on a car in motion. It's a physics problem, a design problem, and a vehicle-level integration problem all at once. The shop prepared me for exactly that kind of thinking.

Today I'm Chief Engineer of WashU Racing, coordinating 60+ engineers across six technical systems on WUFR-27. The work looks different than it did in the shop. The instinct behind it hasn't changed.

As Chief Engineer, my job is to make sure every decision across six engineering sub-teams — aerodynamics, suspension, chassis, electronics and data acquisition, ergonomics, and powertrain — serves the same goal: a faster, more consistent car at competition.

I started from the data. Analyzing our competition results identified suspension and powertrain inconsistencies as the primary drivers of our performance gaps. For engineers — vehicle-level methodology and testing process

Goal-setting and test structure

Goal-setting starts with competition data: lap times broken down by event segment, dynamic event scores, and on-car data from previous seasons. I use this to identify which systems are underperforming relative to their theoretical potential — in WUFR-27's case, suspension inconsistency and powertrain variability were the clearest signals.

Testing plans are structured around falsifiable targets: each system has a defined performance envelope to hit before integration, and the full-car testing sequence is designed to isolate variables before competition loads make the data harder to interpret cleanly.

  • Six sub-teams: aero, suspension, chassis, E&DA, ergonomics, powertrain
  • 60+ members across the full program
  • Full ownership of test plan creation, scheduling, and execution

For engineers — CFD methodology and validation approach

From lap simulation to validated CFD

Aero targets are set using OptimumG lap-time simulation, translating aerodynamic parameter changes into lap time delta. This gives every design decision a quantifiable vehicle-level justification. WUFR-27 targets: CL = -3.2, CD = 1.3, CoP = 40–45% front.

Full-car external aerodynamics are simulated in ANSYS Fluent. Validation is performed against wind-tunnel measurements and on-track instrumented testing — target sub-10% delta across CL, CD, CoP, and the pitch/roll operating envelope.

  • Mounting system redesign: ~7% mass reduction below target, reduced service time
  • Cockpit surface redesign: CL −2%, CD −4% per CFD
  • Full pitch and roll operating envelope characterized
  • Documentation produced for design justification and cross-generation knowledge transfer

Bergstrom Inc.
Jun – Aug 2026
CFD Engineer Intern · Rockford, IL
Photo or diagram — Bergstrom facility, HVAC unit, or workflow schematic

Bergstrom manufactures HVAC and climate control systems for specialty vehicles. The engineering challenge I walked into: every new design required manual CFD analysis that took one to two weeks per iteration. By the time results came back, the design had often already moved on.

I built an automated optimization workflow using ANSYS Discovery and ANSYS OptiSLang that cut that cycle to three to four hours. Engineers could now evaluate dozens of geometric variants in the time it previously took to run one. I applied it to two real products — and wrote a full user manual so the workflow would outlast the internship.

For engineers — the optimization pipeline and measured results

Workflow and results

The pipeline uses ANSYS Discovery for parametric geometry generation and rapid CFD solve, feeding into OptiSLang for design-of-experiments and response surface optimization. The key was structuring the parametric model so geometric variations could be explored without manual mesh rebuilding at each step.

  • Lead time: 1–2 weeks → 3–4 hours
  • Blower-fan HVAC unit — evaporator face-velocity uniformity: 65% → 91%
  • 10-outlet air distribution duct — maximum flow imbalance: 52% → 6%
  • User manual written for adoption by company design engineers

Go-Kart Aerodynamic Optimization
Creighton Senior Capstone
Creighton University · 2023 – 2024 · Autodesk Fusion 360, Autodesk CFD, physical wind tunnel

Before I had formal CFD training, I wanted to understand how body shape drives aerodynamic behavior on a vehicle. For my senior capstone, I 3D-scanned a go-kart, imported the geometry into Fusion 360, and made iterative modifications to the body shape. Each variant was simulated in Autodesk CFD to predict the aerodynamic forces it would generate.

To validate the simulations, I built a makeshift wind tunnel from scratch — because if I was going to claim the CFD was accurate, I needed physical data to compare it to. The results explained the physical phenomena behind the aerodynamic forces measured, and CFD predictions matched the wind tunnel data to within approximately 20%.

The project wasn't assigned with this scope — the wind tunnel was my own addition. It was the first time I formally connected physics intuition, computational simulation, and physical measurement, and it's what pointed me toward aerodynamics as a discipline worth pursuing seriously.

Photo or diagram — go-kart model, wind tunnel setup, or CFD results

Autodesk CFD simulation — go-kart body geometry

For engineers — methodology and what the results showed

Methodology

3D scan of physical go-kart → geometry cleanup and parametric modification in Fusion 360 → steady-state CFD in Autodesk CFD across a range of body shape variants → physical wind tunnel testing of representative configurations for validation.

The CFD results successfully explained the physical aerodynamic phenomena at play — including the relationship between body geometry, flow separation, pressure distribution, and resulting lift and drag forces. Wind tunnel correlation: approximately ~20% delta, which is consistent with the fidelity level of the simulation approach used.

Automated EV Charging Extender
Senior Design — WashU
Washington University · Aug – Dec 2025 · SOLIDWORKS, MATLAB

A mechanical design project to solve a real deployment problem: how do you get a conductive charging coupler from a fixed wall port to the charge inlet of a vehicle that may not be perfectly positioned? The answer is a scissor-lift extension mechanism that reaches 5 feet out and retracts to 6 inches.

The mechanism is driven by a single lead screw — one motor, one axis of motion — with a five-stage scissor-lift geometry achieving a 10:1 extension-to-retraction ratio. Arm geometry was optimized using custom kinematic models in MATLAB, and motor and drive-train specification was validated through static and frictional torque analysis.

CAD render, mechanism diagram, or photo of prototype

SOLIDWORKS model — scissor-lift extension mechanism

For engineers — kinematic model, motor spec, and design constraints

Design and analysis

Arm length, arm count, and deployment angle were optimized using custom kinematic MATLAB models, balancing the structural limits of 3D-printed arm geometry against the target extension ratio and packaging envelope.

  • Extension ratio: 10:1 (5 ft extended / 6 in stowed)
  • Motor: NEMA 23 stepper with 1605 ball screw — specified via static and frictional torque analysis
  • Torque safety factor: 1.0 N·m at 5 lb end-effector payload
  • Validated for compliance with IEC 61439-7 (high-voltage outdoor operation)
